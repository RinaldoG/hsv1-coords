# Alias dictionary: how complete it is, and where it is not

Written 13 November 2026, against `aliases_literature.tsv` as generated by
`src/build_aliases.py`. Regenerate with `python3 src/build_aliases.py`.

## How complete it is

**683 rows. 166 canonical genes. 378 distinct gene-to-alias pairs. 103 distinct
citations. Zero rows without a citation.**

| Measure | Count |
|---|---|

| Canonical genes | 166 |
| Distinct gene/alias pairs | 378 |
| Distinct citations | 103 |
| Citations that are papers | 66 |
| Rows with no citation | 0 |
| Confidence: established | 597 |

By alias type: 316 systematic, 159 functional, 136 protein, 50 molecular weight, 22
other.

**Coverage of the classical inventory is effectively complete. 81 genes are in the
classical inventory and 80 of them carry at least one cited non-systematic alias.** The
single exception is `US1.5`, which has no protein, functional or weight name in the
literature: it is a shorter in-frame gene inside US1 and has only ever been called
`US1.5`. So the classical half of the job is done, not sampled.

Coverage of the 2020 inventory is a different shape. All 85 names the re-annotation added
are present and cited, but almost all of them carry only their own systematic name,
because almost none of them has any other name: they were resolved in 2020 and nobody has
had time to nickname them. That is a property of the inventory, not a gap in the
dictionary.

**Every alias resolves to exactly one canonical gene, with one exception**, `IE63`, which
is recorded as an ambiguity against both UL54 and US1 rather than merged. So the M1
gate's "name to canonical to name is lossless for every entry" holds, with one documented
and deliberate failure. Two further strings, `pri-miRNA-H1` and `LAT 0.7kb`, resolve to
two genes each, but that is not an ambiguity: those features sit inside the inverted
repeats and genuinely exist in two identical copies (`IRL2.5` and `TRL2.5`). Any name in
the repeat regions has this property by construction, which is the same fact that
milestone 4's mask exists to enforce.

The M1 gate asked for the synonymy of at least three published papers to be reproduced.
`alias_testcases.md` holds thirteen, each with its access level stated.

## Where the gaps are

Four real gaps, in descending order of how much they cost.

**1. The foundational papers are unreadable, so the convention origins are attributed
rather than quoted.** The papers that created the ICP, VP and UL/US naming are all from
1972 to 1988 and are either paywalled or held in PMC as page scans with no extractable
text. In each case the abstract establishes the scheme but prints no individual gene name:

- `Honess & Roizman 1973` (PMID 4357511) is the origin of the ICP numbering, and its
  abstract gives "at least 49 infected cell polypeptides (ICP)" but names none of them.
  Worth noting the expansion: ICP is *infected cell polypeptide*, not "protein", though
  both expansions are in print.
- `Spear & Roizman 1972` (PMID 4110104) is the origin of the virion-protein band
  numbering, and its abstract gives "24 bands of proteins and glycoproteins" but prints no
  `VP` label. The capsid numbers first appear as bare numbers in `Gibson & Roizman 1972`
  (PMID 4344252): "four structural proteins, i.e., no. 5, 19, 23, and 24 ... they contain
  proteins no. 21 and 22a in addition". The phrase "virion protein 22" is verified in
  `Gibson & Roizman 1974` (PMID 4129836). **So the band numbers are cited, but the `VP`
  prefix as a printed string in 1972 is not.**
- `McGeoch et al. 1988` (PMID 2839594) introduced the UL numbering and its abstract says
  "Fifty-six genes were identified", naming none. `McGeoch et al. 1985` (PMID 2984429) did
  the same for US with "We consider that this region contains 12 genes". Both full texts
  return 403 with no PMC copy.

The dictionary handles this by citing these papers on the systematic rows at confidence
`probable`, with a note on each row saying exactly what is verified (the scheme and its
extent) and what is not (the printed string). That is the honest reading and it is
visible in the data rather than buried here.

**2. The one source that would settle everything was not read.** Chapter 6 of *Human
Herpesviruses* (Cambridge 2007), Roizman and Campadelli-Fiume, pp. 70-92, NCBI Bookshelf
`NBK47364`, PMID 21348062, is free, authoritative, and contains exactly the table this
dictionary is trying to reproduce. Its own abstract says so: "the table contained in this
section is to summarize the functions of all known gene products". NCBI Bookshelf serves a
CAPTCHA to every automated client, and six access routes were tried. It loads normally in
a browser. **Reading that one chapter by hand is the highest-value remaining action for
this milestone**, and it would likely resolve several of the unverified candidates below.

**3. The *Fields Virology* adjudication is reported, not quoted.** Chapter 59 of the 6th
edition (Pellett and Roizman, from p. 1802) carries an explicit discussion of HSV naming
synonymy that sets out `VP16 = ICP25 = vmw65 = alpha-TIF = UL48` and
`ICP4 = vmw175 = alpha4`. This was read from a scanned copy, in part, without a verbatim
quotation being captured. It is in the dictionary at confidence `probable` with that
caveat on the row. It is simultaneously the strongest adjudicating source in the file and
the weakest-verified citation in it, which is an uncomfortable but accurate state.

Also worth recording because it corrects a reasonable assumption: **the classical gene
inventory in *Fields Virology* 6th edition chapter 60 is a prose list, not a numbered
table.** It runs ORF by ORF under a heading "Open Reading Frames", in the form
"UL56 (234 codons, gamma-2, nonessential)" and "alpha47/US12 (88 codons, alpha,
nonessential)", with aliases inline in the prose. Anyone planning to transcribe "the
Fields gene table" should know there is no such table to transcribe.

**4. The glycoprotein letters have no nomenclature authority to cite.** There is no HSV
glycoprotein nomenclature consensus paper. A targeted search found only the varicella
zoster virus equivalent (Davison AJ et al. 1986, *J Virol* 57:1195-1197, PMID 3005621).
The HSV letters accreted paper by paper, so the dictionary cites discovery papers where it
can (`gK`: Hutchinson et al. 1992, PMID 1323714, "designated gK (the ninth HSV-1
glycoprotein to be described)"; `gL`: Hutchinson et al. 1992, PMID 1312629, "was named
glycoprotein L (gL)"; `gG`: Frame et al. 1986, PMID 3007661) and falls back to database
records for the rest. For `gB`, `gC` and `gD`, the oldest and most-used letters in the
set, the only citations in the file are the reference record and UniProt. That is a
citation, so the hard rule holds, but it is not an origin.

## The two inventories are kept apart, and the `inventory` column is derived

The classical count is **80 ORFs**. The 2020 re-annotation states this three times and
departs from it explicitly. Quoting the paper (Whisnant et al. 2020,
DOI 10.1038/s41467-020-15992-5, PMID 32341360, full text read):

> "The predicted 80 open reading frames (ORFs) of herpes simplex virus 1 (HSV-1) have
> been intensively studied for decades... We identify a total of 201 transcripts and 284
> ORFs including all known and 46 novel large ORFs."

> "The obtained data confirmed the expression of all 80 previously annotated ORFs (CDS)
> and detected 46 additional large ORFs and 134 small ORFs (3-99 aa). We also identified
> 7 N-terminal truncations (NTTs) and 17 N-terminal extensions (NTEs) of CDS. In total,
> our data provides evidence for the translation of 284 viral ORFs."

**The `inventory` column is computed from the citations on each gene-alias pair, not
asserted by hand.** A pair cited only to pre-2020 sources reads `classical`; a pair cited
only to the re-annotation reads `whisnant2020`; a pair attested on both sides reads
`both`. This is why the two are never silently merged: the column is a statement about
evidence, and it cannot drift from the citations beneath it.

**The re-annotation renames nothing.** Its discussion is explicit: "we extended the
existing nomenclature to include these all our novel viral gene products. This did not
involve any renaming of previously described viral gene products." Its supplementary rule
1 names its own baseline: "No previously annotated ORFs were renamed to avoid causing
confusions with previous work. All viral ORFs and transcript mentioned in the 6th Edition
of Fields of Virology were included." So "classical inventory" in this dictionary means,
concretely, the gene list in *Fields Virology* 6th edition chapter 60.

Its naming grammar for new features, from the supplementary methods:

- **Decimal numbering, rule 10:** "Numbering of new identifiers was defined based on the
  location of the TiSS or TaSS in relation to the neighboring previously annotated genes
  (x and x+1) on either strand. In case multiple new identifiers were required between two
  annotated genes, the most strongly expressed gene was named x.5, the neighboring ones
  x.4 and x.6." So `UL20.5` is the most strongly expressed new gene between UL20 and UL21,
  and `UL20.4` and `UL20.6` flank it. **The decimal is an expression ranking, not a
  position**, which is counter-intuitive and worth knowing before anyone sorts on it.
- **Short ORFs:** `uORF` starts and terminates upstream of a large ORF; `uoORF` overlaps
  the main ORF; `iORF` sits inside a large ORF in a different frame; `dORF` sits
  downstream; a bare `sORF` is on a transcript containing no large ORF.
- **Isoforms:** `*n` is an extended transcript initiating upstream, `#n` a truncated one
  initiating downstream, with higher numbers meaning greater distance; `iso1`/`iso2` are
  alternative splices; `in` is a retained intron.
- **`orphan`** marks any ORF whose translation no identified transcript explains, or any
  transcript with no ORF in its first 500 nt.
- **Repeat regions, rule 21:** "ORFs, sORFs and transcripts expressed from the repeat
  regions of the viral genome where named accordingly, e.g. IRL2.5 ORF and TRL2.5 ORF. We
  did not differentiate the three other possible orientations of the unique long and
  unique short regions." **This is the rule behind the biggest naming difference between
  the two inventories**: the classical records annotate `RL1`, `RL2` and `RS1` once each,
  while the re-annotation names both copies separately as `TRL1`/`IRL1`, `TRL2`/`IRL2` and
  `TRS1`/`IRS1`. The dictionary holds all of these, with the two-copy names in the
  `whisnant2020` inventory and the single classical name in `classical`.
- **Rule 2** adds a ` CDS` suffix to every classical ORF name in the deposition
  ("we labeled all previous ORFs as 'coding sequences', e.g. UL1 CDS"). That is a
  systematic string transformation over the whole classical set rather than 80 separate
  aliases, so it is recorded here rather than as 80 rows. A consumer stripping a trailing
  ` CDS` handles all of them.

One practical warning for anyone parsing `BK012101.1`: its `/gene` qualifier holds
**gene-cluster** names, often hyphenated spans such as `UL49-UL50.6` or `IRL2-IRL1`, and
the individual feature names live in `/product`. This dictionary was built from `/product`
and `/note`. A build from `/gene` would produce nonsense.

The re-annotation uses the sequence name `JN555585`, and **gives no reason: it never
mentions `NC_001806` at all.** The practical explanation is that the two accessions hold
byte-identical sequence (confirmed independently in this repo's `FINDINGS.md`) and a
GenBank third-party-annotation record must cite a primary INSDC accession rather than a
RefSeq. That is inference, not the paper's claim, and is marked as such.

## Ambiguities found, and left unresolved

Eight rows are marked `ambiguous`. They are five distinct problems.

### 1. `IE63` has been used for two different genes

This is the real one, and it is the only case where one alias resolves to two genes.

**The HSV-1 reading is UL54 / ICP27, and the evidence is strong.** `McGregor et al. 1996`
(PMID 8627719) opens its abstract "The essential herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1)
immediate-early IE63 (ICP27) is pleiotropic in function". `Mullen et al. 1995`
(PMID 7983744) gives "IE175 (ICP4), IE110 (ICP0), and IE63 (ICP27)". `Perry & McGeoch
1988` (PMID 2846760) places the gene in UL, not US. UniProt P10238 carries "Immediate-early
protein IE63" on ICP27.

**The competing usage is in `BK012101.1`**, whose CDS note on US1 reads `icp22; ie63`,
while its note on UL54 reads `icp27; ie63`. The same authoritative record puts the same
string on two genes.

**A probable origin for the collision was found, and it is cross-species.** `Jackers et al.
1992` (PMID 1316489) states: "Varicella-zoster virus (VZV) gene 63 encodes a protein
(IE63) ... which has amino acid similarities to the immediate-early (IE) protein 22
(ICP22) of herpes simplex virus type 1." VZV IE63 is the homologue of HSV-1 ICP22, so
`ie63` plausibly reached US1 by homology transfer. Targeted searching for an HSV-1 paper
calling US1 or ICP22 "IE63" found none: every hit either uses IE63 for ICP27 with ICP22 as
a separate protein in the same paper, or is a VZV, pseudorabies or Marek's disease paper
discussing VZV IE63.

**It is recorded, not resolved.** Both readings are in the file with their citations, both
marked `ambiguous`, and the US1 row carries the VZV explanation. The correct HSV-1 weight
name for US1 is `IE68` (UniProt P04485) or `Vmw68`. A consumer that must pick one should
pick UL54 and should say that it picked.

### 2. `ICP10` is the HSV-2 name for the HSV-1 `ICP6` protein

`Chung et al. 1989` (PMID 2545912) uses both in one abstract: "The large subunit of the
herpes simplex virus type 2 (HSV-2) ribonucleotide reductase (RR1) ... The HSV-1 RR1 was
shown to be negative for PK activity under these conditions." The HSV-1 and HSV-2 ICP
series were numbered independently by gel mobility, so `ICP6` and `ICP10` are positional
homologues carrying different numbers. `ICP10` is held against UL39 as a cross-species
ambiguity and is not merged into the HSV-1 alias set.

The same shape appears twice more and is flagged on the rows: the only located attestation
of `Vmw68` is in a bovine herpesvirus 1 paper (`Schwyzer et al. 1994`, PMID 8021599,
"homology to ICP22 (Vmw68) of herpes simplex virus type 1"), and the only located
attestation of `Vmw63` is likewise in a BHV-1 paper (`Singh et al. 1996`, PMID 8627249,
"homology to ICP27 (Vmw63) of herpes simplex virus 1"). Both glosses are explicit and
about the HSV-1 protein, so they are usable, but they are not HSV-1 primary papers and the
rows say so.

### 3. `capsid scaffold protein` and `capsid scaffolding protein` are different genes

One letter apart, and they are not synonyms. `NC_001806.2` gives `capsid scaffold protein`
as the `/product` of **UL26.5**. UniProt P10210 gives `Capsid scaffolding protein` as the
recommended name of **UL26**. A string match on either will silently pick the wrong gene.
Both are marked `ambiguous` with a pointer to the other.

### 4. `AP` / assembly protein straddles UL26 and UL26.5

UniProt P10210 names the cleaved "Assembly protein (AP)" chain of UL26. That chain covers
the same polypeptide region as the separate UL26.5 product. So `AP` is attributable to
either gene depending on whether the full-length precursor or the nested gene is meant.
Marked `ambiguous` and not resolved.

### 5. `AL-RNA` is named in the literature and not observed by the re-annotation

`Perng et al. 2002` (PMID 12134005) describes it as a transcript antisense to the 5' end
of LAT. `BK012101.1` records the name and cites that paper in its own note, with the
verdict "not observed". Both facts are in the file: the name is `probable` against the
primary paper and `ambiguous` against the record that looked for it and did not find it.

### A near-collision that is not an ambiguity, but will bite

`VP22` (UL49, a tegument protein) and `VP22a` (UL26.5, the capsid scaffold) are different
proteins of different genes, and the strings differ by one character. Both are in
`NC_001806.2`, which is itself the citation that they are distinct. The separation is
older than the gene assignments: `Gibson & Roizman 1974` (PMID 4129836) already reported
that "protein 22a of B capsids ... does not have an electrophoretically identical
counterpart in virions" and runs at a different mobility from "virion protein 22". The
gene assignments followed much later, UL49 for VP22 in `Elliott & Meredith 1992`
(PMID 1312128) and UL26.5 for the ICP35 family in `Liu & Roizman 1991` (PMID 1845885).
Any dictionary that normalises away a trailing `a` merges two genes.

### A conflict between two authorities that both have Davison on them

`UL49A` versus `UL49.5`, for one gene. The ICTV *Orthoherpesviridae* report chapter's
Table 2 writes `UL49A`. `Davison 2010` (PMID 20346601) writes `UL49.5` in its body text.
`BK012101.1` carries an mRNA with `/product="UL49.5"` and `/note="UL49A"`, stating the
equivalence itself. Both names are in the file, each with its own citation, and the gene
is recorded under `UL49A` as canonical only because that is the reference record's
spelling. Note that `UL49.5` is **not** a 2020 name: `Barker & Roizman 1992`
(PMID 1309263) designated it, 28 years earlier, which is why the dictionary places it in
the `both` inventory rather than `whisnant2020`. The same pair of sources also disagrees
on the core-gene count, 43 in the ICTV chapter against 44 in Davison 2010, so any
statement of that number has to name its source.

Two negative findings worth recording so nobody repeats the work:

- **The ICTV *Herpesviridae* 2021 profile paper (PMID 34704922) contains no gene names at
  all.** Nor does `Davison et al. 2009` "The order Herpesvirales" (PMID 19066710), nor the
  ICTV 9th Report's Herpesviridae chapter. Do not cite any of them for an alias. The ICTV
  *report chapter* is different and is used here: its Table 2 gives the 43 conserved core
  genes with standardised protein names, but no ICP, VP or Vmw name anywhere in the
  chapter, and no US gene at all.
- **NCBI Gene's "Also known as" field is not a synonym source for this organism.** For all
  80 live HSV-1 gene records it contains only the locus tag (`HHV1gp00pNN`). A full
  `efetch` of GeneID 2703392 (RS1) confirms `Gene-ref_syn` is empty. The useful naming is
  in the `description` field instead, which is the same string as the RefSeq `/product`.
  Worse, the 48 *discontinued* records do carry second gene names (GeneID 2703418 UL49 /
  "UL49A"), but they are keyed to the superseded `NC_001806.1` and are offset, so they are
  a trap rather than a source.

## Deliberate scope exclusion

The LAT-region microRNAs (`miR-H1` to `miR-H8` and their `-3p` / `-5p` arms) are **not
given canonical entries**. Two `pri-miRNA` names are included where `BK012101.1` carries
them as feature notes, and `Umbach et al. 2008` (PMID 18596690) and `Cui et al. 2006`
(PMID 16699030) are cited for LAT itself. A full miRNA naming layer is a separate
dictionary with its own naming authority (miRBase) and its own ambiguities, and folding it
in half-done would be worse than leaving it out. `alias_testcases.md` test case 10 is
where this gap is visible, since that paper uses eleven `miR-H` strings.

## Is a citation-per-alias dictionary achievable for the full gene set?

**Yes for the classical inventory, and it is done. No for the 2020 inventory, and that is
not fixable by more work.**

**The classical half is achieved.** 80 of 81 classical genes carry at least one cited
non-systematic alias, and the one exception has no such name to find. The multiply-named
genes, the ones where the problem actually bites, are the best covered: UL48 alone carries
twelve distinct names, its systematic one plus eleven aliases across four conventions,
with three independent papers glossing them together. The hard rule held without a single exception being needed: 683 rows, zero
uncited.

But "achieved" needs a qualification that the `confidence` column carries honestly. 78
rows are `probable` rather than `established`, and most of those are the systematic names
cited to the McGeoch papers, where the numbering scheme is verified from an abstract and
the individual printed string is not. **So for the classical set the dictionary is
complete at the level of "every alias has a real, findable source", and incomplete at the
level of "every alias has been seen on the page where it was coined".** The second
standard is reachable for perhaps another twenty rows by reading four specific
inaccessible sources, and is probably not reachable at all for the 1972 to 1974 band
numbering, which exists in print as page scans that may never be text-extracted.

**The 2020 inventory cannot meet the same standard, for a structural reason rather than an
access one.** The re-annotation added 46 large ORFs, 134 small ORFs and dozens of
transcript isoforms, and gave them names generated by a documented rule rather than
assigned by a describing paper. `UL20.6` is not a name somebody coined for a gene they
characterised; it is the output of rule 10 applied to an expression ranking. There is
exactly one citation available for each of those 85 names and there will never be a
second until somebody studies the gene and names it. This repo's earlier research
(`PLAN.md`, from strand `03`) already recorded that the 46 novel large ORFs have no
interaction data and appeared in no screen, which is the same fact from the other
direction.

So the honest answer is that **citation-per-alias scales with how long a gene has been
interesting, not with how many genes there are.** For the roughly 30 genes that carry
protein, functional and weight names, the dictionary can be and now is densely cited, two
to five independent sources per alias. For the remaining 50 classical genes it is singly
or doubly cited, usually to the reference record and the ICTV core table. For the 85 novel
2020 features it is singly cited to the annotation that created them, and that is the
ceiling.

**What this means for M1's gate.** The gate is met: name to canonical to name is lossless
except at one recorded ambiguity, thirteen published papers' synonymy is reproduced
against thirteen named test cases, and there are zero rows with an uncited alias. What the
gate does not and should not claim is that every alias has been read in the paper that
coined it. The `confidence` column is where that distinction lives, and a consumer
filtering to `confidence = established` gets 597 rows that will survive a specialist
reading them.
